EPISODE 1

Callum Reyes woke up at 4:45 a.m. The way he did every morning: to a phone alarm in a bedroom the size of a shipping container, staring at a water stain on the ceiling that over two years had slowly grown into the shape of a cock and balls. His landlord called it "character." Callum called it a mould problem.

He lay there for sixty seconds because sixty seconds was how long he allowed himself to hate his life before he had to go and live it.

The city of Port Merrow didn't really have sunrises anymore. It had a smog-glow, an orange smear where the refinery stacks coughed their daily load into the sky and under it sprawled the grey mess: warehouses, tenement blocks, rail yards and chimneys. Thousands of chimneys. You could count the trees left in the city on one hand. The council had cut the last big one down to make a car park for a bank.

Breakfast was two slices of white bread, margarine scraped so thin you could read through it and instant coffee made with tap water that ran brown for the first ten seconds. Then he checked his bank app, out of masochism.

$212.47.

Payday was Thursday. Rent was due Friday: $380 a week for a unit with mould, a broken heater and a neighbour who screamed at his PlayStation through the wall at 2 a.m. Payday would cover rent and then absolutely nothing else a maths problem Callum had been failing to solve for three years.

On the windowsill sat the living thing in the apartment: a supermarket basil plant that was mostly dead. He watered it anyway. His nan had kept a vegetable patch out the back when he was a kid. He'd spent every summer weekend on his knees in the dirt beside her. It was the time he could remember feeling exactly where he was supposed to be. He'd wanted to be a landscape gardener. Then he'd found out what the course cost and what gardeners got paid and what rent cost, and the dream had died quietly in a corner like everything.

* * *

Halloran & Voss Metalworks squatted at the edge of the district like a big ugly toad with the brand new logo of its parent company, Voss Industrial Group, glowing proudly on the roof.

Callum worked on Line 4, the stamping line. A sheet of steel came down a conveyor. He guided it into a four-hundred-tonne press. The press came down with a boom that rattled his fillings, and out came a car door panel. Then he did it again. Nine hundred times a shift, twelve-hour shifts, five days a week, six when Dennis asked "nicely."

Dennis Pratt, the floor supervisor, spoke exclusively in company slogans. "We're a family, Callum." "Voss is investing in YOU." "Overtime is voluntary. We remember who volunteers." Quarter the floor had hit its target, and management had celebrated with a pizza party. One slice each. Corporate had made forty-one million dollars. The people who'd made it got a slice of Hawaiian and a laminated certificate.

The wage was $19.40 an hour. Bathroom breaks were timed. The break room microwave hadn't worked since March, and above it hung a poster reading SAFETY IS EVERYONE'S RESPONSIBILITY, right where Old Tomasz had left two fingers in a feed roller in 2019. He'd been back at work within a week. Nobody had said sorry. Somebody had put up the poster.

Callum was twenty-nine. Moved like he was fifty-five. "Don't think about it ", he muttered, nine hundred times a shift. "Just feed the press."

* * *

That night Dennis had asked nicely.

By 11 p.m. Callum had been on his feet for sixteen hours, so he spent his seven-minute break on a pallet in the loading yard behind the plant, where the air only tasted of metal. It was a wasteland of cracked concrete and oily puddles under one flickering sodium lamp, with refinery flares burning beyond the fence against a sky dirty for stars. He ate half a sandwich and tried to remember the last time he'd felt anything but tired.

"Fuck me ", he said to nobody. "Is this it? Is this the whole thing?"

The air in front of him folded.

It was like someone had grabbed a corner of the night and pulled. A line of green-gold light split the darkness three metres off the ground. Tore open like a wound made of sunlight. The sodium lamp. Died.. In a yard that had smelled of oil for forty years, Callum smelled rain on warm earth, cut grass and something sweet he hadn't smelled since his nan's garden.

A vine unspooled from the tree, thick as his arm. Lowered itself to the concrete in lazy coils. Then a boot appeared. Then another. A tall, bony, scruffy old man in a patched green robe walked down the vine like a staircase, with a beard to his belt buckle that had actual leaves growing out of it, a pointed hat that looked like it had lost a fight with a lawnmower and a gnarled staff in one hand. In the other, an eaten pear.

He stepped onto the concrete. Everywhere his boots touched, flowers bloomed.

They burst up through the cracks, through the oil, through forty years of grime: bluebells, daisies, tiny white starflowers, a carpet of colour racing across the yard until the skip bin wore a crown of morning glories and the barbed wire had gone green with ivy. The vine above blossomed into a hundred lanterns of light and the whole yard glowed like a goddamn festival.

The old man took a bite of his pear. Looked around at the chimneys, the burning refinery and the sad little sandwich on the ground.

"Gods ", he said. "What a shithole."

Then his pale, sharp eyes landed on Callum, who was still frozen against the bin with his mouth hanging open.

"You ", said the wizard, pointing the pear. "Callum Reyes, is it? Excellent. You look like shit, lad. Come on. We've got work to do."
